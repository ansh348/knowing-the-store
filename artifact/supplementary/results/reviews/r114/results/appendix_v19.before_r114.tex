\section{Question-signal audit}\label{app:questionprior}

This appendix records that the question text alone predicts the labels.
That is why no absolute blind level in this paper is a finding, and why the paired gains and the counterfactual set carry the evidence.
Supervised text probes separate \pn{} from non-\pn{} far above chance on every question set, while a zero-shot GPT-4.1 mini reader does not.
The larger $162$-question comparison stays separable (Table~\ref{tab:questionprior}).
The records we deleted were removed by the store's own operation, yet the questions written about them still read differently from the rest.
The structure runs deeper than authorship, because ranking by the recipe used to build each item alone scores $0.9736$ on the cross-model set, above the probe's $0.7972$ (Table~\ref{tab:av-census}).
Six of the eight recipes carry a single class, so rewording cannot make blind levels clean.
Separating construction from authorship needs comparison within a recipe, and only one is large enough ($n=16$).
The same holds for coverage: on hard questions the FOK-positive class coincides with \pn{}, and the coverage-targeted probe scores $0.9662$ pooled, so blind coverage levels carry the same question signal.
Two quantities are free of this signal, because neither depends on the wording of a question.
The paired gains compare identical questions and still measure the summary's added information, and the counterfactual set fixes the question and varies the store (Table~\ref{tab:counterfactual}).
The balanced authorship-matched set ($36$) holds every slot at the same one-third \pn{} rate, and blind NOK is at chance on it for GPT-4.1 mini and Opus 5.

\subsection{Authorship-matched set}\label{app:matched}

The authorship-matched set rewrites every question in one generator voice from each item's entity and slot, so no label is distinguishable by who wrote its question.
It also balances the set so that each slot token carries the same one-third \pn{} rate (Table~\ref{tab:matched-cell}).
Coefficient inspection of the supervised probe first suggested an authorship mismatch, with \pn{} questions bespoke and many negatives reusing generator templates.
The census of Table~\ref{tab:av-census} shows the structure runs deeper than authorship, because six of its eight recipes carry a single class.

%%V11:1083-1108%%

This set is not a store counterfactual, and what it holds fixed is stated here because the two designs are easy to confuse.
Within a slot the question is near-identical across labels, differing only in the entity named, and the slot's \pn{} rate is one third by construction.
That is what makes it an authorship and blind-score control.
Each item in a slot belongs to a different persona, so the label co-varies with the entire store rather than with the target's lifecycle state alone.
A counterfactual in the strict sense scores one query against versions of the same store differing only in the target's lifecycle state, which is the design of Appendix~\ref{app:counterfactual}.

\paragraph{PN versus PRESENT, and the ordering within triples (post hoc, specified before recomputation).}
The pooled obligation AUROC of Table~\ref{tab:cf-main} scores the \pn{} versions against both other versions, so it mixes two distinctions: \pn{} against \present{}, where both versions hold relevant content, and \pn{} against \absent{}.
A reader that only knows whether the store covers the question scores $1$ on \pn{} and \present{} and $0$ on \absent{}, which gives $0.75$ pooled on this set and $0.5000$ on the \pn{}-versus-\present{} comparison, because it ties every triple.
Table~\ref{tab:pnpresent} separates the two for every reader, summary and state, with the pooled value equal to the mean of the two (asserted to four decimals, the negative groups being equal in size), and counts, per triple, whether the reader scores the \pn{} version above, equal to, or below the \present{} version.
The largest move from the pooled value on the whole set, any reader, is $0.0230$, so the model-versus-lookup comparison of Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual} is the same on the harder comparison: the name lookup reads $0.7663$, GPT-4.1 mini $0.7056$, Haiku 4.5 $0.7493$ and Opus 5 $0.8370$.
The strict-win fraction counts ties as failures and is not a tie-adjusted ranking accuracy; GPT-4.1 mini's single call returns coarse probabilities, so its tie count is large ($57$ of $148$ triples on the whole set), and the interval to read is the persona-grouped one, since triples share personas.
Where the summary only counts a state, the two lookups tie every retraction and expiry triple under the superseded-only summary (their lists do not differ), the count readers win most of them, and the models split between ties and small numbers of wins and losses; naming the states turns most of those ties into wins for every reader.
The expected outcomes written before the run held for the name lookup and GPT-4.1 mini (supersession above $0.85$ and $0.70$; retraction and expiry within tolerance of $0.50$ under the superseded-only summary) and for the all-states lookup (above $0.70$ on both counted states); they did not hold for Opus 5, which reads $0.5689$ on retraction and $0.6128$ on expiry against the \present{} versions, nor for Haiku 4.5 on expiry ($0.5544$), nor for the count readers, which the pooled result had already shown to separate the counted states.

\paragraph{GPT-4.1 mini on the expiry pairs under the all-states summary.}
Under the all-states summary GPT-4.1 mini reads retraction at $0.8725$ and expiry at $0.5869$ on this set, although both states are named in the same way.
The $30$ expiry pairs show why, because the three versions of each pair can be read side by side.
The model scores its \pn{} version at or below $0.05$ on $26$ of the $30$ pairs, and the \present{} and \absent{} versions score the same low values, so the three versions are indistinguishable to it.
Only four pairs draw a score of $0.40$ or more on the \pn{} version, and in two of those the \present{} version draws the same score.
Every expiry question carries the upcoming intent, and every \pn{} version names the expired attribute in the expired-names section.
The model therefore does not take a name listed under the expired heading as evidence against a question about an upcoming event, whereas a name under the retracted heading moves it.
Appendix~\ref{app:resp} records the same asymmetry on the full population, where the expiry row falls under the all-states summary.

\subsection{Statistics and serialization}

% C142
FOK AUROC treats non-\absent{} coverage as positive, while NOK AUROC treats \pn{} as positive.

% C143
Reported $95\%$ confidence intervals on paired AUROC differences use the fixed scorers' paired percentile bootstrap \citep{efron1993}, preserved in the anonymous artifact.
Within each reported row, $1{,}000$ item-level resamples are drawn with replacement under fixed seed $42$, with paired-condition scores moving together.
AUROC differences are recomputed per draw, single-class draws are discarded, and the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of retained differences form the interval.
A whole row containing one outcome class has undefined AUROC and no interval, so the mechanism probe reports mean coverage for such external-backend strata.
Ladder one-sample AUROC intervals use the same item-level draw-and-discard rule, so the scored item rather than the persona is the implemented resampling unit.
A persona-clustered sensitivity \citep{cameron2008}, resampling whole personas under the same paired draw-and-discard rule, was computed after the item-level records were frozen.
The preregistered item-level interval remains the primary endpoint.
The measured intracluster correlation of the paired indexed$-$blind contribution spans $[-0.0544,+0.0870]$, and the clustered-to-jackknife width ratio is $0.95$--$0.99$ on all six calibration rows.
Two independent cluster-level variance estimators therefore agree, and four of six clustered intervals are narrower than their item-level counterparts (Table~\ref{tab:cluster-sensitivity}).

\paragraph{A fourth backend, measured on three personas.}
A trial on an unmodified public release of a fourth backend, Graphiti \citep{zep}, a temporal knowledge-graph store built by another group, asks whether the self-description problem is confined to the three backends above.
On three personas and $137$ episodes it names at least one superseded attribute per persona, so its summary is not degenerate.
Against the persona manifests it recovers $2$ of $10$ supersession events at the $0.80$ overlap bar this study inherited, a recall of $0.20$.
At the $0.75$ and $0.50$ bands the same census recovers $5$ and $6$, and three of the ten events sit between $0.75$ and $0.80$.
The verdict therefore turns on a threshold rather than on a wide margin, and all three bands are reported.
What it does mark is mostly not a manifest event, because precision runs $0.00$ to $0.04$ across the three personas.
Retraction and category are inexpressible under its stock defaults, so the summary emits the marker for both.
Three properties of the store account for most of the misses.
The user is split across three entity nodes in every persona, so a later fact about the same slot does not meet the earlier one.
About a tenth of edges carry no validity time, and invalidation requires one on both sides, and some temporal bookkeeping is incoherent, with one edge invalidated before its own validity begins.
The failure mode is erasure with overstatement in what survives, the same direction as FR-Bank's at a far smaller absolute scale.
Because it covers three personas it is a trial rather than a census, and no obligation judgment is scored on this backend.
Disclosure, because it bears on independence: the personas and episodes used here were ingested into that backend in early 2026 under a copy carrying four documented numeric-preservation modifications.
Re-running the unmodified release on the same personas and episodes changes edge counts by $2.4\%$ and leaves supersession recall identical at the frozen bar, so those modifications are near-inert on this corpus.
Since the trial, the census was run on all forty personas (Appendix~\ref{app:posthoc-graphiti}); the summary route and the tag route on this backend have not been run.

\paragraph{Retrieval that returns tags, as configured.}
The retriever is the store's own cosine search over every entry the item's store variant holds, active or not, on the bank's cached embedding vectors.
It applies no lifecycle mask, no lexical channel, no category forcing, no multi-hop and no distillation, so every entry can be returned; the top ten are taken and the top five used.
Each returned line carries the lifecycle flags the store's ground-truth join records, the category, the scrubbed attribute, the age in days, and the text.
That is a modification of FR-Bank's stock configuration, which masks non-active records, and it is what makes the tags a route at all.

\subsection{Calibration after the fact}\label{app:posthoc-cal}

The elicited score is rescaled by three monotone maps \citep{platt2000,zadrozny2002,guo2017} under leave-one-persona-out cross-validation on the pooled hard-tier coverage set.
That set holds $588$ questions.
No question therefore contributes to the fit that predicts it.
Expected calibration error on the intact-summary score falls from $0.1741$ to $0.0146$ under an isotonic map and $0.0029$ under a logistic one.
That comparison needs its floor, and the floor is a constant predictor.
At a base rate of $5.95\%$ a constant predictor scores an expected calibration error of $0.0000$ and a Brier score \citep{brier1950} of $0.0560$.
The calibration numbers alone therefore understate how little is being asked.
Against that floor the rescaled score improves the Brier score to $0.0519$.
It also improves selective risk \citep{geifman2017} from $0.0615$ to $0.0373$, which is where its resolution shows.
Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} is the reliability diagram behind these numbers.

%%V11:2161-2177%%

The rescaled probability never exceeds $0.375$ on the intact summary or $0.450$ on the names-only summary, so the value at which it would cross one half does not exist on either.
The comparison is therefore reported at each population's own base rate, where the crossings sit at $0.3$ and $0.2$ in score space.
At that threshold the rescaled intact-summary score flags $134$ of the $588$ hard questions and catches $24$ of the $35$ covered ones, a precision of $0.1791$ and a recall of $0.6857$ under the isotonic map.
Precision is low because the map's probabilities crowd the base rate, so the threshold that recovers most positives admits more than four false alarms for each true one.
Ordering is unchanged by construction, since every map is monotone.
Monotonicity is checked on each fitted map rather than on the pooled out-of-fold values.
Those pooled values are a mixture of forty maps, so their pooled ordering statistic moves.
On the question-only condition it reads $0.2572$ against a raw $0.4946$, a diagnostic of the pooling rather than a property of any map.

\subsection{A disclosure: the counterfactual set split by whether the question names its attribute}\label{app:posthoc-flags}

We read every question of the counterfactual set before scoring and flagged it when it names the plan or value its attribute is about (Appendix~\ref{app:counterfactual}).
The flag belongs to the question, so it is identical across the three store versions and cannot carry the label.
Table~\ref{tab:flag-split} splits the $444$ items by that flag, $89$ questions flagged and $59$ not, and reports every reader on each half.

It does not: every reader scores higher on the questions that name only the topic, the name lookup at $0.8668$ against $0.7142$.
